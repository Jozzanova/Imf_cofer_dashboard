Behind the change

A change in the US-dollar amount includes valuation: when the dollar strengthens, reserves held in other currencies are worth fewer dollars even if no central bank sold any. Shares move for the same reason.

Data, definitions & caveats
  • COFER (Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves) is the IMF's survey of the currencies central banks hold their foreign exchange reserves in. Countries report to the IMF in confidence; the IMF publishes only the world totals shown here. Foreign exchange reserves exclude gold, SDRs and the reserve position in the IMF.
  • Shares are percentages of allocated reserves: those whose currency composition is known. In the current release unallocated reserves are zero from 2000 on; in 1995–1999 they were about a fifth of the total (shown grey in Amounts). From 2025 Q3 the IMF also reports the part of total reserves whose composition it has imputed (estimated) itself, about 10%.
  • Amounts are in US dollars at each period's market exchange rates. A change in an amount therefore mixes what central banks bought or sold with valuation: when the dollar rises, reserves in other currencies are worth fewer dollars. The same holds for shares.
  • Yearly figures are the end-of-year positions (the fourth quarter). Before 1999 there are only yearly figures, and the euro's predecessors (Deutsche mark, French franc, Dutch guilder and the ECU) are shown combined as Euro predecessors.
  • Other currencies is everything outside the eight currencies the IMF lists separately, for example the Korean won, the Singapore dollar and the Nordic currencies. The renminbi is listed separately from 2016 Q4, the Australian and Canadian dollars from 2012 Q4; before then they are part of other currencies.
  • Live data: each time the page opens it asks the IMF's API for the current COFER release, so new quarters and revisions appear without rebuilding it. If the IMF cannot be reached, the page shows the copy it was built with. The IMF publishes COFER once a quarter, about three months after the quarter ends, and revises earlier figures when countries revise theirs.
