Currency moves or central banks' choices?

A currency's share of reserves changes for two reasons: central banks buy or sell it, or its exchange rate against the dollar moves. Holding every exchange rate at its level on the start date separates the two.

Data, definitions & caveats
  • COFER (Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves) is the IMF's survey of the currencies central banks hold their foreign exchange reserves in. Countries report to the IMF in confidence; the IMF publishes only the world totals shown here. Foreign exchange reserves exclude gold, SDRs and the reserve position in the IMF.
  • Shares are percentages of allocated reserves: those whose currency composition is known. In the current release unallocated reserves are zero from 2000 on; in 1995–1999 they were about a fifth of the total (shown grey in the US-dollar chart). From 2025 Q3 the IMF also reports the part of total reserves whose composition it has imputed (estimated) itself, about 10%.
  • Amounts are in US dollars at each period's market exchange rates. A change in an amount therefore mixes what central banks bought or sold with valuation: when the dollar rises, reserves in other currencies are worth fewer dollars. The same holds for shares.
  • Yearly figures are the end-of-year positions (the fourth quarter). Before 1999 there are only yearly figures, and the euro's predecessors (Deutsche mark, French franc, Dutch guilder and the ECU) are shown combined as Euro predecessors.
  • Other currencies is everything outside the eight currencies the IMF lists separately, for example the Korean won, the Singapore dollar and the Nordic currencies. The renminbi is listed separately from 2016 Q4, the Australian and Canadian dollars from 2012 Q4; before then they are part of other currencies.
  • Explore: the race ranks the currencies every quarter. Currency moves or central banks' choices values each currency's holdings at the exchange rates of the start date (end-of-quarter rates from the IMF's Exchange Rates dataset): the difference between the actual share and that share is the effect of exchange rates; the rest is the change in holdings. "Other currencies" has no single exchange rate and stays at current values. Purchases and sales are estimated the same way, quarter by quarter, at the average of the opening and closing exchange rates. Both are estimates: changes in which countries report, and the IMF's own revisions and imputations, also count as "holdings".
  • Countries: each country's official reserve assets from the IMF's International Investment Position statistics (foreign exchange, gold at market value, SDRs, the reserve position in the IMF and other reserve assets), quarterly where the country reports quarterly and otherwise at year-end, divided by that calendar year's GDP in US dollars from the IMF's World Economic Outlook (the latest year is an IMF estimate). Norway's GDP is Statistics Norway's, after its main revision of the national accounts in November 2025, which raised the level of GDP by about 4% (for 2023). Sources from before it show a lower GDP, such as about US$ 483 bn for 2024 against 501 bn now, and so higher reserves as a share of GDP. Reserves are a stock and GDP a yearly flow, the usual way to compare them. "Latest available" uses each country's most recent figure, if it is no more than two years old. Where the International Investment Position has no gold figure (or gives next to none where the IRFCL has a billion dollars or more, as for Türkiye, whose reserve assets there also leave it out), the quarter's or year-end gold at market value from the IMF's IRFCL statistics is used (Norway's gold, sold in 2004, is only there); failing both, a country's last reported amount is assumed. Countries that are not IMF members (such as Taiwan) or do not report are missing. These are national totals, not their currency composition: the IMF publishes that only for the world as a whole. Advanced and emerging economies and the regions are the World Economic Outlook's groups. Exchange-rate regimes are measured from end-of-month exchange rates over the last two years (see the note under "Compare groups"); with monthly data a few currencies can land in a neighbouring category to the IMF's official one. For the groups over time, each year uses the two years of exchange rates up to its end (from the IMF's full monthly history; before 1999 the Deutsche mark, at the euro's conversion rate, stands in for the euro), and euro-area members count as floating from the year they adopted the euro. Advanced and emerging economies and regions use today's IMF groups for every year.
  • Live data: each time the page opens it asks the IMF's API for the current COFER release, so new quarters and revisions appear without rebuilding it. If the IMF cannot be reached, the page shows the copy it was built with. The IMF publishes COFER once a quarter, about three months after the quarter ends, and revises earlier figures when countries revise theirs.
